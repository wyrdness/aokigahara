## Overview

Aokigahara (青木ヶ原), also called Jukai (樹海, 'Sea of Trees'), is a roughly 30 km² (≈12 sq mi) forest at the northwestern foot of Mount Fuji in Yamanashi Prefecture, Japan. The forest grew on the Aokigahara lava flow produced by the 864 CE Jōgan eruption of Mount Fuji's flank vents (in particular Mount Nagao). Its substrate of porous basalt, weak compass-disturbing magnetic anomalies in places, dense undergrowth and acoustic dampening have given the forest a reputation for disorientation. Aokigahara is associated in Japanese folklore with yūrei (restless ghosts) and is the setting of Seichō Matsumoto's 1960 novel 'Tower of Waves' (Nami no Tō), which is widely credited with shaping its modern reputation. From the late 20th century onward Aokigahara has become widely known internationally as a site of suicide; Japanese authorities and volunteer organisations conduct annual sweeps and have installed signs encouraging visitors to seek help. In 2017 the forest gained additional global notoriety when American YouTuber Logan Paul uploaded a video showing a deceased person filmed inside the forest, prompting widespread condemnation and changes to YouTube's policies. Conservationists, mental-health advocates, and local communities increasingly object to sensationalised 'suicide forest' framings, emphasising Aokigahara's ecological, geological, and cultural significance.

## Etymology

- **Origin**: Japanese 青木ヶ原 (Aokigahara): 'aoki' (青木) is the green-leaved shrub Aucuba japonica; 'ga' is a possessive particle; 'hara' (原) means 'plain' or 'field'.
- **Meaning**: Approximately 'Aokigi (Aucuba) plain'; commonly rendered loosely as 'sea of green trees'. The compound Jukai (樹海) means 'sea of trees'.
- **First use**: Place name long established in Edo-period and modern Japanese gazetteers.

## Theories & Explanations

### Paranormal / Cryptozoological

**Yūrei haunting** _(Japanese folklore tradition)_

Folkloric framing in which the forest is haunted by yūrei, the restless dead, with the forest atmosphere amplifying perception of supernatural presence.

### Skeptical

**Reputation effect** _(Mental-health professionals, Mental-health charities in Japan)_

Aokigahara's reputation has been amplified by literature (Matsumoto 1960), self-help / reference works (Tsurumi 1993), and global media (Logan Paul 2017), creating a feedback loop that drives further visits and reporting.

**Geophysical disorientation** _(Earth-science commentators)_

Reports of compasses misbehaving and easy disorientation are partly explained by localised magnetic anomalies in basalt and by the forest's acoustic dampening and uniform terrain.
